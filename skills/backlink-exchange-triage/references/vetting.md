# Vetting a link-exchange offer

## 1. Signs of a link seller or network

One sign alone proves little. Three or more together almost always mean an agency or freelancer selling links, whatever the subject line says.

**In the sender:**
- A free-mail address (gmail.com, outlook.com, proton.me) with "seo", "outreach", "backlinks" or "offpage" in the name, or a person's name plus a number.
- A company domain that doesn't match the signature, or one unrelated to the topic (for example, a construction or gaming company pitching a "software link exchange").
- An "outreach specialist" or "link builder" title with no company site of its own.
- Mail-tracking footers (for example "Sender notified with Mailtrack").

**In the offer:**
- "ABC", "3-way", "A-B-C", or "one of my sites" or "my partner sites": the link you'd get is on a different site from the one getting your link.
- A list of 5+ unrelated domains to choose from, or "DR 50+ / 70+, 1K+ traffic" offered generically.
- "GP/LI", "niche edits", "do-follow permanent links", "your rates and guidelines", "turnaround time".
- Asking you to name the anchor and page before saying which of their pages it would go on.
- "Paid or exchange, whichever you prefer"; a discount request; "regular orders"; "I can pay $X".
- The same template sent again with a new subject, 3-6 follow-ups in 2 weeks, and a final "last nudge".
- An offer of a big list of "partner sites" or "social media promotion" instead of a link from their own site.

**What a real offer usually looks like:**
- A named person at the company whose site gives the link (the founder, the content or marketing lead, a PR lead), writing from that company's domain.
- It names a specific article of yours and suggests a specific resource of theirs, often with a draft sentence.
- It's clear about which page of theirs your link would go on, or offers to let you pick.
- An agency writing for a client can still be fine (deal shape 1) when the client's own site gives the link back.

## 2. The gates, in more detail

- **Off-limits niche:** the user's blocklist, plus anything the user's readers would find odd on their site ("if a reader would scratch their head, so would Google").
- **"Covers everything" sites:** categories for tech, health, finance, travel, pets and real estate all at once; "write for us" pages in several niches; 30+ outbound links in a short post. These sell links.
- **Not a real site:** thin pages, mostly AI text, a blog with thousands of posts and no authors, nothing posted in the last 12 months, or no version in the user's language.
- **Deal shape the user doesn't accept:** payment in either direction, 3-way swaps (unless allowed), link networks, footer, sidebar or "partners page" links, or a link on a page the user didn't agree to.
- **Already linking, or already declined:** check the user's log, and a free backlink checker if one is to hand.

## 3. The score, in more detail

**Reader fit (0-2).** Compare the partner's readers with the user's readers from the config, not just the topic words. A software company's blog read by small-business marketers can fit a marketing tool well, even if the products differ. A "tech news" site read by anyone fits nobody in particular. 0 here is a skip at any authority.

**Real authority (0-2).** Use a free checker (section 4) for Domain Rating or Domain Authority, and look for real organic traffic. High DR with tiny traffic usually means bought links. A small, focused site with modest DR and real readers can still be worth it, so note it and let the user judge rather than cutting it automatically.

**Page fit (0-2).** Find the actual article where the user's link would go (section 5). Score 2 only if you can name the URL and the paragraph.

**Deal shape (0-2).** 2: direct and 1-for-1. 1: through an agency, but 1-for-1 between the two sites concerned. 0: 3-way, network, or unclear.

**Freshness (0-2).** Old, quiet threads rarely revive. But a thread where the other side said "yes, pick your spot" and is waiting on the user is a 2 however old it is. Say so in the report.

## 4. Free authority checks

**With a browser** (setup question 10), open one domain at a time:
- Ahrefs Website Authority Checker: `https://ahrefs.com/website-authority-checker?input=<domain>`. It shows DR, backlinks and linking websites in a pop-up; take a screenshot or zoom on it.
- Moz Domain Analysis (free with an account): Domain Authority and top pages.
- Semrush (free account, a few lookups a day): Authority Score and estimated traffic.

If a checker shows a bot check or a daily limit, stop and switch tools; don't solve it. Note where each number came from in the report ("DR 51, Ahrefs free checker"). Paid tools that spend credits need the user's OK first.

**Without a browser** (web search and page fetching only), most free checkers won't load, because they need JavaScript. Use what you can read:
- **Web search** for the domain: does the brand show up for its own topic, does it have reviews, press or a real company behind it? A site that only shows up in "write for us" lists is a warning sign.
- **The site itself:** its sitemap (how many posts, how recent, how regular), its blog dates, its About and team pages, and any "advertise" page or media kit, which often states traffic.
- **Signs of real readers:** comments, a newsletter, social accounts with real engagement, a product people actually use.

Mark authority as "not checked" (score it 1 if everything else looks real, 0 if anything looks off), say so in the report, and give the user the checker link for each domain so they can look it up in a few seconds.

## 5. Finding the right page on the partner's site

- Read their sitemap (`/sitemap.xml`, `/sitemap_index.xml`, or the URL listed in `/robots.txt`). Filter the URLs for the user's topic words and read the 2-3 best matches.
- Prefer an article that is indexed, dated in the last 12-18 months, and already talks about the problem the user's page solves.
- Find the earliest natural spot: the first real section after the intro. Avoid summaries, tables of contents and the conclusion.
- Write 1 sentence that helps their reader even without the link, in the article's voice. Put the link on a short phrase that says what the user's page is.
- Check the user's side too: confirm the user's page that would carry the partner's link exists, and that the partner's resource fits there.

## 6. Replies to an offer that's half right

- **"Which site would our link be on?"** Ask before doing anything when the sender is an agency or the email says "on our end".
- **3-way, when the user allows it:** say plainly which site gives and which gets each link, and ask for the giving site's article URL.
- **They want a guest post and the user doesn't take them:** offer a 1-for-1 link instead, in 1 line.
- **They name a fee:** don't negotiate in a draft. Report it to the user and stop.
